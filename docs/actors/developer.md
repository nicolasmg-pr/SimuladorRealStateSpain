# Developer

Residential developers/builders (promotores y constructores) in Spain. Researched 2026-08-07.
All figures fetched and verified on that date unless labelled (unverified). Source register:
`docs/sources.md` (rows staged in scratch `sources/developer.md`).

## 1. Role & size in the market

- **Output 2025**: 139,016 new-dwelling permits (visados de dirección de obra nueva), +8.8%
  vs 127,721 in 2024. Of these, 112,533 multi-family (en bloque, +10%) and 26,430
  single-family (+4.3%). Source: MIVAU, via elEconomista/Forbes/idealista (2026-02).
- **Completions 2025**: 88,254 dwellings with certificado fin de obra (CFO) per MIVAU
  (−10% vs 97,837 in 2024; first year since 2014 with CFO falling in all four quarters).
  Banco de España's Informe Anual cites ~92,000 completed in 2025. **Record both: 88k–92k**
  (definitional/vintage difference between CFO counts and BdE's stock-growth estimate).
- **Demand benchmark**: net household formation ~240,000/yr (2025, BdE). Completions cover
  roughly 37–38% of household formation. Cumulative deficit 2021–2025 ≈ **750,000 dwellings**
  (BdE, June 2026), half concentrated in 6 provinces: Madrid, Barcelona, Alicante,
  Valencia, Murcia, Málaga — i.e. the model's tensioned-metro zones.
- **Historical amplitude**: peak **865,000 visados in 2006**; trough 2013 (22,316 in H1-2013;
  full-year ≈34,000 (unverified precise figure)). Peak-to-trough ≈ −96%. Current output is
  ~16% of the 2006 peak.
- **Industry structure**: highly fragmented. Top-8 developers ≈ **18% of new-build output**
  (elEconomista, 2021 data). Top-10 delivered 21,375 units in 2024 (+38.6% y/y); largest is
  Aedas Homes with 5,211 deliveries. The long tail is small local promoters and
  self-promotion (autopromoción; ~19% of 2025 visados are single-family).
- **VPO/protected housing**: ~**16.7% of housing starts** were protected (Oct-2024–Sep-2025,
  idealista/MIVAU). 2024: 14,371 definitive calificaciones (+62%, highest since 2014) and
  23,967 provisional (+94.8%). Context: >85k/yr in 1997, 68,587 in 2008, under 10k/yr for
  most of the 2010s. Recovering from a historic trough but still ~79% below 2008.

## 2. Balance sheet & constraints

- **Financing model (post-2008)**: equity for land purchase (banks rarely finance land),
  then a préstamo promotor for construction: typically **50–80% of cost (LTC)** — 65–75%
  for experienced developers, 50–65% for novices; drawdowns against work certifications;
  interest-only during construction; Euribor-12M + 200–450 bp (effective ~3.5–6% in
  2025–26). Sources: Banco Santander product page, BdE Cliente Bancario, broker guides
  (prestamopromotor.com, xenia-cap).
- **Pre-sales requirement**: banks demand **30–50%+ of units pre-sold** before releasing
  construction finance. This makes supply demand-gated: projects only start where
  purchasing demand is already demonstrated. Alternative lenders accept lower pre-sales at
  higher rates. (Two independent broker/bank sources.)
- **Cost structure (national, 2025)**:
  - Hard construction cost, multi-family, contract execution: **1,105 €/m²** (UVE
    Valoraciones, 2025, +3.66% y/y). ACR/idealista measure a broader construction cost of
    **1,323 €/m²** (Sep-2025). Cumulative rise **+32% (2020–2025, UVE)**; +25% since 2019
    (idealista, Jul-2026); structure/shell work +39.8% (2019–2025).
  - Land: **25–50% of final price** depending on zone. CNMC: "up to 45% of final cost".
    Press/industry: Madrid >50% claimed, ~35% considered the healthy ceiling, 20–30%
    typical outside tensioned areas. Use zone-differentiated range.
  - Soft costs (licences, taxes, fees, technical, marketing): residual ~10–20% of cost
    (unverified as a single series; consistent with promoter viability guides).
  - Gross developer margin: **15–30% over cost**, with **15–20% margin-on-cost as the
    viability threshold**; target equity IRR 15–25%; equity investors demand 12–15%
    (Urbanitae/KPMG via Alimarket; IMPLICA and promoter-finance guides).
- **Labour constraint**: construction employment collapsed from ~2.7–3.0M (2007) to
  ~1.1–1.2M (2013–14) and never recovered; average worker age 37.3 (2007) → 45.1 (2022).
  CNC (employers' confederation — industry viewpoint) claims **~700,000 additional workers
  needed** to scale output; sector target ≥220,000 homes/yr, "stable ~150,000/yr in the
  medium term". Treat the 700k as an industry claim; the ageing/scarcity itself is
  corroborated by UVE's cost decomposition and BdE's supply-restriction diagnosis.
- **Land constraint**: converting raw land to buildable plots (suelo finalista) takes
  **10–15 years, up to 20** — CNMC (independent regulator): planning 7–11 yrs + urban
  management 2–5 yrs + building; APCE/Colliers (developer association) give the same
  10–15–20 range. Rare case where regulator and industry agree. APCE claims only ~1% of
  planned land is ready to build (unverified, industry claim via COPE).

## 3. Observed decision rules (with evidence)

1. **Start rule (margin threshold)**: launch a project only if expected sale price ≥
   land + hard cost + soft cost + **15–20% margin on cost**. Evidence: viability guides
   (IMPLICA), promoter-finance market practice, Urbanitae/KPMG report. This creates a
   **price floor for new supply**: in zones where market prices sit below all-in cost
   (~1,700–2,200 €/m² of sale price given 2025 costs — derived, medium confidence), no
   private new build occurs. Matches observed geography: output concentrates in the six
   high-price provinces while rural Spain gets almost no new build.
2. **Demand gate (pre-sales)**: construction start is conditional on 30–50% pre-sales
   (bank requirement). ABM implication: starts respond to *transacted* demand at the
   posted price, not to price levels alone; this dampens speculative overshoot relative
   to pre-2008 but slows the supply response.
3. **Land banking**: post-2008 developers buy almost exclusively suelo finalista and hold
   multi-year land banks; listed developers "shield" results with pre-sales (€4.2bn
   pre-sales book, elEconomista 2025). Land purchases are the true supply decision, made
   3–15+ years before delivery.
4. **Price-signal response is slow and inelastic**: long-run supply elasticity ~0.45–0.58
   (see §6). Even with prices at all-time highs (2024–25), visados grew only 8.8% in 2025
   and completions *fell* ~10% — a supply response constrained by land, labour and permit
   throughput rather than by will.
5. **VPO participation**: developers build VPO when land reserves/public incentives make
   it pencil (calificaciones doubled in 2024 off a historic low); it is a policy-driven,
   not price-driven, decision channel.

## 4. Reaction to past shocks (case episodes)

- **2008–2014 collapse**: visados fell from 865k (2006) to ~34k (2013), −96%. Martinsa-
  Fadesa filed the largest bankruptcy in Spanish history (Jul-2008, €7.2bn debt); most of
  the developer sector was liquidated or absorbed; construction employment fell by
  ~1.5–1.8M. **Capacity was destroyed, not idled**: firms, skilled crews and the
  land-development pipeline all disappeared. This is why the industry could not simply
  re-scale in the 2020s.
- **2015–2019 rebuild**: new institutional developers (Aedas, Neinor, Vía Célere,
  Metrovacesa relaunch) created on international capital, buying finalist land from
  banks/SAREB. Output recovered to ~100k visados/yr — an order of magnitude below the boom.
- **2021–2025 demand surge, muted supply response**: household formation ~240k/yr and
  record prices, yet visados only reached 139k (2025) and completions fell. Competing
  explanations — record ALL, don't adjudicate:
  - *Land*: suelo finalista exhausted in tensioned metros; 10–20 yr pipeline (CNMC and
    APCE agree on timelines; APCE emphasises this most — industry viewpoint).
  - *Labour*: shortage + ageing workforce (CNC claim of 700k gap; corroborated
    qualitatively by cost indices and BdE).
  - *Costs*: +25–32% construction cost since 2019/2020 compressed margins, making
    mid-price segments unviable (UVE, ACR).
  - *Permits/administration*: 8–18 month licence waits and fragmented regulation (APCE
    data; CNMC calls Spanish land regulation among the most restrictive in the OECD).
  - BdE's synthesis: structural supply restrictions, not demand policy, are the binding
    problem.
- **2021–22 materials shock** (post-COVID + Ukraine): structure/shell costs +39.8%
  2019–2025; developers repriced or paused projects rather than absorb losses —
  consistent with the margin-threshold rule.

## 5. Power & relations to other actors

- **vs Bank**: dependent. Pre-sales and LTC conditions transmit credit policy directly
  into starts; bank risk appetite is a supply-side lever in the model.
- **vs Government (3 levels)**: municipalities control licences (8–18 months) and
  planning; regions control VPO calificaciones and land law; the state sets housing plans.
  Developers lobby via APCE/CNC for finalist land release, faster licences and tax help —
  treat their public numbers as advocacy (Tier-2, industry viewpoint).
- **vs Households**: developers sell mostly to owner-occupiers with pre-sale contracts
  (price fixed ~24 months before delivery); pre-sales shift price risk to buyers.
- **vs Investors**: institutional investors fund build-to-rent and buy turnkey blocks;
  equity funds demand 12–15% returns, setting the hurdle rate.
- **vs Landowners**: in tensioned zones, scarce finalist land lets landowners capture
  much of the price upside (land share up to 45–50% of final price) — developers describe
  ceding margin to land sellers.

## 7. Open questions

1. **Arrazola et al. (2015) numeric supply elasticity** — abstract says supply is "highly
   sensitive" to prices, which conflicts with the 0.45 consensus. Need the full PDF to
   extract the coefficient. If materially above 0.58, widen the model's elasticity range
   and treat 0.45 as the short/medium-run value.
2. Is BdE's 0.45 an independent estimate or a citation of Caldera & Johansson? If the
   latter, we effectively have one primary estimate plus one dissent — thin evidence for
   the single most important developer parameter.
3. **Zone-differentiated costs**: no official construction-cost or land-share series by
   zone type found. Zone multipliers in §6 are guesses; look for tasadora (Tinsa/ST) or
   ACR regional breakdowns.
4. **Soft-cost share** (taxes, licences, fees) lacks a citable series; AEAT/municipal
   data or an APCE cost breakdown (checked against a non-industry source) would firm it up.
5. **Completions discrepancy** 88,254 (MIVAU CFO) vs ~92,000 (BdE): confirm definitions
   before using as a validation target.
6. **How binding is each constraint by zone?** Land (metros), labour (everywhere),
   permits (specific municipalities) — the ABM needs to know which constraint caps starts
   in which zone type; current evidence identifies the candidates but not their relative
   weights. BdE provincial deficit data could help.
7. **Trough precision**: full-year 2013/2014 visados (~34k) not verified against the MIVAU
   series directly — pull the raw MIVAU table.
8. **Pre-2008 decision rules differed** (land speculation, 100%+ financing, no pre-sales
   discipline). If the model is calibrated on post-2015 behaviour, do not use 1997–2007
   dynamics for validation without a regime switch.
