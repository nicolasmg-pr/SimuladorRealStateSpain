# Investor — large (grandes tenedores, SOCIMIs, funds)

Researched 2026-08-07. All URLs fetched/searched on that date. Source register:
scratch `sources/investor-large.md` (to be merged into `docs/sources.md`).
Tier-1 = raw statistics/legal text; Tier-2 = interpretive reports, always
triangulated across opposing viewpoints (tenant unions vs. industry vs.
central bank vs. academia).

## 1. Role & size in the market

**Legal definition (Tier 1, BOE).** Ley 12/2023 por el derecho a la vivienda,
art. 3.k: a *gran tenedor* is any natural or legal person owning **more than 10
urban residential properties** or **>1,500 m² built residential area**
(garages/storage excluded). In a declared *zona de mercado residencial
tensionado* the region may lower the threshold to **5+ properties located in
that zone** (must be justified in the declaration memoria). Verified against
the consolidated text at boe.es (BOE-A-2023-12203).

Policy levers that apply **only** to grandes tenedores:
- **Rent cap on new contracts in tensioned zones** (Disposición Final 1ª,
  amending LAU 29/1994): when the landlord is a gran tenedor, the new rent may
  not exceed the applicable reference-index cap (small landlords are only bound
  by the previous-contract rent + indexation; grandes tenedores are bound by
  the index itself). Applied in Catalonia from 16 Mar 2024 (140 municipalities,
  extended to 271 covering >90% of the Catalan population from Oct 2024).
- **Eviction procedure obligations** (DF 5ª, amending LEC 1/2000): mandatory
  conciliation/mediation before eviction when claimant is a gran tenedor and
  the occupant is vulnerable; suspension periods; in practice regions demand a
  social-rent offer before proceeding.
- **Information duties** (Título I): grandes tenedores in tensioned zones must
  report on the use/occupancy of their dwellings on request.
- **ABM note**: the 10/5-unit threshold is a *behavioural discontinuity* —
  investors just below the threshold escape the cap entirely.

**Size — estimates disagree; keep the range.**
- Banco de España (Informe Anual 2023 cap. 4; Documento Ocasional 2432, read
  directly from the PDF): **~8% of market-price habitual rental dwellings
  owned by private legal entities** vs >90% by individuals (2021 data); BdE
  describes a "modest relative weight of the institutional private sector".
- Fundación Civio (2024, transparency-law data; disputes BdE methodology):
  **16%** of rental contracts held by entities with 50+ contracts; **10%** for
  private companies only.
- Atlas Real Estate Analytics (consultancy, 2026): grandes tenedores hold
  **≈110,000 rental dwellings ≈5% of rental supply**; press (La República,
  Aug 2026, transparency-law data) reports top-10 owners >200,000 units and
  "large holders" rising from ~3% of stock (2010) to 9.2% (2026) —
  definitions differ across these counts.
- ASVAL / industry framing: <5% of stock in funds' hands; ~40 investors share
  ~4.2% of rental stock — far below Germany/Netherlands institutional
  penetration.
- **Working range for the model: strict institutional (SOCIMIs+funds) 2–5% of
  rental stock; all legal entities ~8–10%; all 50+-contract holders ~16%.
  Heavily concentrated in Madrid/Barcelona metro cores (i.e., exactly the
  tensioned zones).**

**Named portfolios** (Atlas via Infobae, Apr 2026; tenant union claims higher):
Blackstone ~20,000 (Sindicat de Llogateres: up to 40,000 incl. all vehicles;
~13,000 Madrid, ~5,500–6,600 Catalonia), CaixaBank ~20,000, Cerberus ~6,300,
Azora/Nestar ~5,600, CEVASA ~2,200, Ares ~1,300. Blackstone's five SOCIMIs
(Testa, Albirana, Fidere, Torbel, Euripo) held ≈30,000 units at peak
(eldiario.es); the "residential SOCIMIs ≈35k units" figure often cited is
consistent with a **30–40k peak range, shrinking since 2021** through
disposals (Fidere −5,000 in 2026; Testa selling unit-by-unit).

**Share of purchases** (Tier 1: Consejo General del Notariado / BdE DO 2433):
legal persons ≈**10% of dwelling transactions** since 2020 (12% avg 2014–2019;
~20% 2012–2014 during the distressed cycle; record absolute count 76,745 in
2024). Institutional funds proper are a subset of that 10%.

**Build-to-rent pipeline**: ~90,180 units projected to 2028 (Atlas, incl.
public plans — announcements, not deliveries); Madrid took 72.2% of 2020–24
BTR investment vs Barcelona 8.1% (Savills). Flex-living stock ~19,100 units
(2025), pipeline ~16,000 (Cushman & Wakefield). BTR promotion in Barcelona
"practically halted" by rent regulation (C&W via idealista, Jun 2026).

## 2. Balance sheet & constraints

- **SOCIMI regime** (Ley 11/2009 as amended): 0% corporate income tax (plus a
  15% charge on undistributed profits since 2021; press sometimes reports
  "1%"), mandatory distribution of ≥80% of rental profits as dividends, 95%
  ITP/AJD rebate, listing requirement. The regime survived the 2024 PSOE–Sumar
  agreement to abolish it (PSOE backed off Nov 2024 after Merlin/Colonial
  threatened redomiciliation) — abolition risk is now a standing political
  risk premium on Spanish residential.
- **Leverage**: SOCIMI/fund LTVs typically 30–50% (core vehicles low end,
  opportunistic higher). (unverified — no hard series fetched; Confidence low.)
- **Cost of capital**: prime residential yields of 3.0–3.25% in 2022 could not
  compete with the 10-y Bono near 3% in 2023 (CBRE) — forcing ~60–85 bps yield
  decompression. Core unlevered IRR targets ~7.5–8% (US CBRE analogue);
  opportunistic funds target ~15%+ levered IRR (unverified for Spain; standard
  mandates).
- **Regulation, not financing, is the binding constraint** for this actor:
  reference-index caps, 3%/IRAV indexation limits, eviction delays and the
  5-unit tensioned-zone threshold all bind grandes tenedores specifically.
- Closed-end fund structures force exits regardless of policy: observed
  Spanish hold periods 7–13 years (Fidere 2013→2026; Testa 2018→2024–26;
  Albirana ~2015→2021+).

## 3. Observed decision rules (with evidence)

1. **Buy distressed portfolios at deep discount; sell retail at market.**
   Entry 2013–2017 at 31–63% haircuts (Sareb transfer terms, Tier 1) and
   ~half local market price (EMVS flats ~€67,200/unit ≈ €970/m² vs ~€2,000/m²
   in Vallecas — Reuters 2014; idealista 2022). Exit 2021–2026 unit-by-unit
   ("piso a piso") to owner-occupiers, capturing the retail premium (idealista
   Jun 2026; vozpopuli). The portfolio-vs-retail spread is a core ABM
   mechanism (~15–25% — unverified, Confidence low).
2. **Yield-hurdle entry rule**: acquire when stabilised net yield clears cost
   of debt + risk premium. Prime multifamily net yields: Madrid 3.0–3.25%
   (2022) → 3.6% (end-2023) → 3.8% (Q4-2024 through Q1-2026); Barcelona
   3.85% → 4.0% (CBRE). Small-investor gross yields for contrast: Madrid
   4.7–5.4%, Barcelona 5.6–6.7%, Spain 6.7–7.3% (idealista 2025-26); BdE RBA
   ~5.6% avg 2011–2022, ex-post total return 6.3% nominal p.a. (10.5% p.a.
   2015–2022 incl. capital gains).
3. **Exit/reallocate when rent control binds.** Evidence chain: Albirana sold
   ~1,500 units 2021–mid-2022 citing "inseguridad jurídica" and the Housing
   Law (idealista/elEconomista, Jan 2023); Testa (~8,300 units) selling
   unit-by-unit; Fidere (~5,000 units, 47 buildings) sold to Brookfield for
   €1,050M ≈ €210k/unit (idealista, Mar 2026) — note Fidere is a transfer
   *within* the institutional sector, not stock leaving the rental market.
   Capital rotated from BTR to build-to-sell, student housing (PBSA) and
   flex-living (C&W). Tenant-union counter-reading (Sindicat de Llogateres):
   sales also harvest a decade of price gains, and funds used managers
   (Anticipa, Alquilovers) to refuse renewals during COVID decrees — so treat
   "regulation caused exit" as jointly caused by regulation + fund maturity.
4. **Regulatory arbitrage into exempt segments.** Seasonal (temporada) and
   room rentals sit outside LAU caps: seasonal contracts +45% in Catalonia in
   2024 and +52% y/y to 11% of Q1-2025 signings (Incasòl data via press);
   room-rental supply +40% y/y Q1-2024, with Barcelona/Madrid/València holding
   ~60% of room supply (BdE DO 2432); tourist+seasonal ≈45% of Barcelona
   rental offer mid-2023 (Observatori Metropolità de l'Habitatge). Catalonia
   closed the loophole 18 Dec 2025 (caps extended to seasonal/rooms); first
   data: −1,233 seasonal contracts (Infobae, Apr 2026). Arbitrage responds
   within quarters, in both directions.
5. **Price-setting**: concentrated exactly in tensioned urban cores (Atlas),
   large landlords are reference-setters in their micro-markets; under caps
   they price AT the cap. García-Montalvo/Monràs/Raya (2020-21 episode):
   rents fell ~5% at the expensive end while *rising to the ceiling* at the
   cheap end — the index acts as a focal price/magnet. Tenant unions document
   above-cap renewal increases; companies deny. Model: asking rent =
   min(unconstrained optimum, cap), cap attracts from below.

## 4. Reaction to past shocks (case episodes)

**2012–2015 distressed entry (Sareb window).** Sareb created 2012, absorbed
€50.78bn of assets with haircuts of 31–63% on housing and 58–88% on land
(Tier 1, transfer terms). Jul–Aug 2013: Madrid city sold 1,860 EMVS social
flats to Blackstone/Magic RE (~€67,200/unit) and the region sold 2,935 IVIMA
flats to Goldman Sachs/Azora (€201M ≈ €68,500/unit) — both ~50% of local
market prices (Reuters; eldiario.es; idealista). Blackstone built its
Anticipa/Albirana rental platform on Catalunya Banc's mortgage book bought
2014-15 (widely reported; details unverified). Sareb itself was majority-
nationalised via FROB in 2022, with servicing outsourced to Blackstone and
KKR's Hipoges; wind-down deadline 2027. **ABM lesson: institutional demand is
countercyclical — it switches on when prices sit 30–60% below peak and public
or bank sellers are forced.**

**2020 Catalan rent cap (Ley 11/2020, Sep 2020 – Mar 2022, struck down by the
Constitutional Court).** Academic evaluation (García-Montalvo, Monràs, Raya —
UPF/BSE WPs, CEPR column): average rents **−5%**, concentrated in expensive
units; price increases toward the cap at the cheap end; exit of supply at the
top not offset by entry below; implied rental supply elasticity ≈4. The
Barcelona housing observatory (O-HB) contests the supply effect (finds
effectiveness without significant supply loss). Both sides agree measured
rents fell. Record as a range, not a verdict.

**2022–23 rate shock.** Total Spanish RE investment −36% in 2023 (€11.2bn,
EjePrime); Living segment −30% (CBRE); prime residential yields decompressed
+60 bps in 2023 (Madrid 3.6%, Barcelona 3.85%). BUT affordable-focused BTR
*grew* +29% in 2023 (€1.87bn) — public-partnership income streams were the
rate-shock refuge. By Q1-2026 living investment had rebounded (+98% y/y,
€2.3bn, 36% of all RE investment) with prime yields stable at 3.8%/4.0%.
No distressed selling was observed — leverage was low enough to hold.

**2024–26 Ley 12/2023 application in Catalonia.** Regulated-zone rents −3.7%
(Apr–Dec 2024), Barcelona −6.4% (Incasòl, Tier 1); new habitual contracts
−19.7% y/y in Q2-2025 (Barcelona metro >−21%); habitual contracts fell from
167,842 (2021) to 108,057 (2025), ≈−36%; seasonal contracts +45–52% until the
Dec 2025 closure; BTR promotion in Barcelona halted; grandes tenedores selling
piso a piso. The Generalitat itself concedes habitual rental is "losing
strength" (idealista, Jul 2026).

## 5. Power & relations to other actors

- **vs Government**: organised lobbying via ASVAL (founded 2020, chaired by
  ex-UN-Habitat head Joan Clos; ~6,000 members incl. Blackstone's Anticipa —
  tenant unions call it "the vulture lobby") and via listed SOCIMIs (Merlin/
  Colonial redomiciliation threats killed the 2024 tax reform). Effective veto
  power at national level; much weaker in Catalonia, where tenant unions set
  the agenda.
- **vs Tenants/unions**: Sindicat de Llogateres runs targeted campaigns
  against named funds (collective renewal bargaining, direct action) and has
  extracted negotiated renewals from Blackstone. Large landlords face
  *collective* tenant power and reputational cost that small landlords do not
  — an extra operating cost in Barcelona specifically.
- **vs Banks/Sareb**: bought their distressed books 2013–2017; Blackstone and
  KKR's Hipoges now service Sareb's remaining assets — funds are simultaneously
  counterparty and contractor of the public sector.
- **vs Small landlords**: regulation is asymmetric (index cap and eviction
  duties bind grandes tenedores harder), so when caps bind, stock migrates
  from large owners to small owners and owner-occupiers; BdE: individuals
  added ~100k rental dwellings/yr on average 2012–2022 while institutions
  stayed marginal.
- **vs Developers**: forward-purchase BTR pipelines (Madrid periphery, Málaga,
  Zaragoza); when caps bind, the same capital flips to build-to-sell / PBSA /
  flex-living, so this channel transmits rent regulation into the tenure mix
  of new supply.

## 7. Open questions

1. **Zone-level shares.** The 2–8% national range hides the split the ABM
   needs: plausibly 8–15% of rental stock in tensioned metro cores vs <1%
   rural, but no source gives this directly (Civio's municipal transparency
   data could — needs a data pull).
2. **Exit destination.** How much "exit" leaves the rental market (piso a piso
   to owner-occupiers) vs merely changes institutional hands (Fidere→
   Brookfield)? No series separates the two; it determines the supply response
   to rent caps.
3. **Leverage and refinancing schedules** of Spanish residential vehicles —
   no hard LTV/maturity data found; governs forced-sale behaviour under rate
   shocks. Testa/Vivenio BME Growth filings would give this.
4. **Premium or discount vs small landlords** for comparable units? Tenant
   unions claim premium + aggressive renewals; BdE shows professionals
   concentrated in low-income, high-yield areas (composition effect).
   Unresolved — run both as scenarios.
5. **Threshold gaming**: do owners split holdings across vehicles to stay
   below 10/5 units? Widely alleged, no quantitative evidence found.
6. **Post-Dec-2025 seasonal cap in Catalonia**: one early data point (−1,233
   seasonal contracts). Does that stock return to habitual rental or exit to
   sale? Too early to say.
7. **Tax-shock elasticity**: SOCIMI-regime abolition stays on the political
   agenda; the exit response to a tax shock (vs a rent-cap shock) has never
   been observed in Spain.
