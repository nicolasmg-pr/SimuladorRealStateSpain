# Investor — small landlord

Status: researched 2026-08-07. Sources register: see `docs/sources.md` and the research
scratch table (Tier 1 / Tier 2 rows) produced with this dossier. Every behavioural claim
below carries ≥2 independent sources; conflicting estimates are kept as ranges.

## 1. Role & size in the market

The Spanish private rental market is owned by individuals, not institutions — this actor
is the supply side of the rental market.

- **Ownership share.** Companies (personas jurídicas) owned only **8.1%** of dwellings
  rented as habitual residence in 2021 (AEAT + Catastro data, in Banco de España DO 24/32);
  Catalonia 11.1%, Madrid 10.3%. The Government's own COVID decree states "in **85%** of
  housing tenancies the landlord is a natural person, a small owner" (RDL 11/2020 preamble);
  an EMVS study put it at 88.8% (2018); press summaries of the BdE Annual Report 2023 cite
  **~92%** of rental dwellings held by individuals/small owners. → Individual share:
  **85–92%** (range kept; sources use different bases: contracts vs dwellings vs habitual-
  residence rentals).
- **How many landlords.** AEAT IRPF statistics: **2.15M** taxpayers applied the reduction
  for renting out housing in 2022 (9.5% of all declarants; El País/AEAT), rising to
  **2.24M** in 2024; **3.26M** declared net income from any rented/ceded property (2024).
  AEAT microdata used for the state rent-reference index covers **~2.5M rented dwellings**
  owned by individuals per year (BdE DO 24/32). Survey-based estimates put landlord
  households at **3–9% of households** (CIS, ECV, OMHB; Uppsala/IDRA-aligned study).
- **Portfolio concentration — dual structure.** Most landlords are small, but most rented
  *stock* is not held by single-unit landlords:
  - Barcelona (OMHB 2020): landlords with 1 unit = **79% of landlords but 38.7% of stock**;
    2+ units = 21% of landlords, **60.3% of stock**; >15 units = 1.2% of landlords, 24.1%
    of stock.
  - Spain (Ministerio de Consumo/CSIC, Household Panel 2023): **52.8%** of individually
    offered rental units belong to multi-landlords (2+ units); multi-landlords + companies
    + public bodies hold ~61% of the stock.
  - Individuals owning **>10 rental dwellings hold at most 7% of the market rental stock**
    (AEAT 2021, in BdE DO 24/32).
  - Mean dwellings per landlord-taxpayer ≈ 2.5M dwellings / 2.15M declarants ≈ **1.2**
    (spouses declare shares separately; per household it is higher — CIS surveys of
    continuous landlords found means of 4–5, small sample).
- **Alternative uses held by the same actor**: ~350,000 tourist dwellings (early 2024)
  ≈ 1.8% of principal dwellings but **~10% of the size of the residential rental market**;
  seasonal (1–11 month) rental listings reached **13% of all rental supply** nationally,
  >40% in Barcelona (BdE DO 24/32; INE experimental statistic).

## 2. Balance sheet & constraints

- **Who they are (EFF 2022, BdE):** 36.1% of households own real-estate assets other than
  their main home; 23.3% own at least one other dwelling. Ownership peaks at head-of-
  household age **55–74** and rises steeply with income and wealth. Median value of other
  real-estate holdings: €120,200.
- **Income and wealth (Consumo/CSIC on EFF 2022 microdata):** landlord households' median
  income **€50,959**/yr (multi-landlords €80,375) vs €32,120 for owner-occupiers and
  €21,335 for tenant households. Median net wealth: landlords **€407,975**, multi-landlords
  €996,826, tenants €2,217. ECV 2021 gives consistent numbers (landlord mean disposable
  income €46,725). Landlords would remain the highest-income group even without rental
  income (tenant-side study on ECV 2021) — rental income is a top-up, not subsistence,
  for the median landlord; but AEAT data show 38% of landlord declarants have total
  income <€21,000, so a sizeable minority does depend on it.
- **Rental income scale:** mean declared net rental income per declarant ≈ **€4,600–6,000**/yr
  (AEAT 2022–2024; higher in Madrid ~€6,000, Cataluña ~€5,500; €2,500–3,000 in Extremadura/
  the Castiles).
- **Leverage:** low. Only a small share of households carry debt on non-main-home property
  (EFF 2022; median outstanding €63,000 among those who do). The typical unit is inherited
  or bought outright — so the 2022–23 rate shock hit opportunity cost, not cash flow.
- **Constraints:** no scale economies or risk pooling (BdE contrasts them with institutional
  landlords who can absorb operating/tax costs and risk with lower premia); one non-paying
  tenant is a large income shock; recovery of a dwelling after default takes **~12.5 months
  on average (380 days, SEAG 2026)**.

## 3. Observed decision rules (with evidence)

- **Yield comparison vs alternatives.** BdE (DO 24/32): gross rental yield (RBA) on the
  rented stock averaged **5.5%/yr (2011–2022)**; entry yields on new contracts **6.5–7.5%**.
  Ex-post total return (rent + price appreciation) 2015–2022: **10.5–10.8%/yr**, vs 10-yr
  government bonds 1.2%, bank deposits 0.3%, IBEX-35 incl. dividends 6.8% — this spread is
  BdE's explanation for the "notable increase in individuals' investment in rental
  housing" over the decade. Idealista (portal, ask-price based): gross yield Spain 7.2%
  (end-2024) → **6.7%** (end-2025); Madrid 4.7%, Barcelona 5.6%, secondary capitals ~7–7.5%
  (Lleida 7.5%, Murcia 7.4%). Portal yields run above BdE stock-based RBA — keep both.
- **Risk premium by zone.** RBA is systematically higher in low-income areas (range ~3–9%
  across municipalities >50k; decile analysis in BdE DO 24/32): BdE reads this partly as a
  **perceived non-payment risk premium** charged where tenants are poorer.
- **Tax treatment shapes the rent-vs-tourist margin.** IRPF net-income reductions
  (Ley 12/2023, from 1-1-2024): **50% general**, 60% if renovated in prior 2 years, 70%
  first-time rental to 18–35-year-olds in tensioned zones (or social/assessed rental), **90%
  if new contract in a tensioned zone cuts rent >5%** vs previous contract; contracts
  pre-26-5-2023 keep the old **60%**. Tourist and seasonal lets get **no reduction**
  (TEAC doctrine) yet still expanded — gross-income differences dominate the tax wedge in
  tensioned zones. In RBA terms: deductible expenses ≈ −2.0pp; the fiscal benefit is worth
  +0.5 to +1.25pp; net IRPF drag on RBA 0.3–0.8pp (BdE/AEAT).
- **Non-payment: perceived ≫ actual.** Actual: ~**25,540 evictions in 2025**, 18,317 for
  rental non-payment (CGPJ; −10.9% yoy) ≈ **<1% of tenancies/yr**. Insurer Arag: **5–7%**
  of contracts show significant non-payment; insured portfolios ran ~3.6% claims pre-COVID
  (OESA). Tenant-side: ECV — tenants with arrears in last 12 months 10.8% (2019) → 16.3%
  (2023); IDRA survey: only 8.5% ever late (Madrid 9.8%, Barcelona 7.2%) — "impago is a
  myth" framing. Perceived: **61% of landlords perceive growing legal insecurity**
  (onLygal 2026 survey); property chambers report "high distrust in the efficiency of
  judicial eviction" (El País 2026). Mean defaulted debt: €3,022 (SEAG) to €8,490
  (Alquiler Seguro/Observatorio del Alquiler, 2025) — landlord-side sources disagree by 3×;
  insurers' mean payout €3,299 (UNESPA 2023–24). → Model perceived risk as a mark-up over
  actual default probability, not equal to it.
- **Substitution margins when regulated.** When caps bind, small landlords (a) reprice up
  to the cap if previously below it (Monràs & García-Montalvo find price *increases* at
  the bottom), (b) exit the top of the market, (c) shift to exempt segments: seasonal
  contracts in Catalonia +52% yoy after the 2024 cap, from 6.1% to 11% of signed contracts
  in one year (Incasòl/Generalitat); national seasonal listings +55% yoy mid-2024 while
  residential listings fell >15% (portals, in BdE DO 24/32); rooms listings +40% yoy.
  Stated intentions (onLygal): with legal backing 50% would do conventional rental, 16%
  tourist, 9% seasonal, 2% rooms.
- **Rent-setting within contracts** is regulation-bound: CPI-linked (RDL 7/2019), capped
  at 2% (2022–23), 3% (2024), INE reference index from 2025. Most price dynamism happens
  at rotation: new-contract prices rose 7.5–10%/yr on average 2015–2022 vs ~2% within
  contracts (BdE).

## 4. Reaction to past shocks (case episodes)

- **2008–2014 bust.** Average rents fell only −7.5% nominal (−10.9% real) while house
  prices fell −22.6% nominal: yields *rose* through the bust and households moved savings
  into rental housing — the individual-owned rental supply "grew substantially over the
  last decade" (BdE DO 24/32). Small landlords behaved counter-cyclically: buy/hold in the
  bust, harvest yield in the recovery. Ex-post returns were negative 2011–2014 (price
  losses), then 10.5%/yr 2015–2022.
- **COVID-19 (2020–ongoing tail).** Eviction suspension for vulnerable tenants without
  housing alternative (RDL 11/2020), plus extraordinary contract extensions and rent
  moratoria vs large holders; repeatedly extended — currently to **31-12-2026**
  (RDL 2/2026) — with a state compensation mechanism for affected landlords
  (RDL 37/2020, RD 401/2021). From 2026 the suspension **no longer applies to landlords
  owning ≤2 dwellings** — explicit regulatory recognition of small-landlord exposure.
  Landlord-side data: claims on uninsured lets ~15% (peaks 20%) in 2020 vs ~3.6% insured
  pre-COVID norms (OESA — landlord/insurer viewpoint, likely upper bound). Lasting effect:
  mass uptake of non-payment insurance and tenant scoring (El País 2026), i.e. a permanent
  rise in screening intensity rather than exit.
- **2022–23 rate rise + inflation caps.** Update cap of 2% then 3% cut real within-contract
  rents while market rents rose ~9–12%/yr (Incasòl, idealista): widened the sitting-vs-new
  tenant gap and raised the option value of rotation/exempt segments. Deposit and bond
  yields rose from ~0 to >3%, compressing the rental spread — coincides with the drop in
  residential listings and the seasonal-rental boom (BdE; portals). Low leverage muted any
  cash-flow channel.
- **Catalonia rent cap #1 (Sep 2020 – Mar 2022, struck down) — THE key natural experiment,
  three conflicting studies, all retained:**
  1. **Monràs & García-Montalvo** (FRBSF WP 2023-28; CEPR 2025): rents **−5%** in treated
     municipalities; new rental contracts (supply) **−10%**, exit concentrated in
     high-price units, not offset at the bottom; implied supply elasticity
     Δln(contracts)/Δln(rent) ≈ **2**.
  2. **Jofre-Monseny, Martínez-Mazza & Segú** (RSUE 2023): rents **−4% to −6%**;
     **no significant supply effect** (neither listings nor contracts).
  3. **Pérez García** (arXiv 2602.08631, evaluating the 2024 national cap in Catalonia):
     rent effect ~−3pp in baseline DiD but **not robust** (PSM-DiD and synthetic DiD ≈ 0);
     tenancy agreements **−13%** (−5 to −7 per 10,000 inhabitants), robust across
     specifications.
- **Barcelona / Catalonia cap #2 (Ley 12/2023 zones, from Mar 2024).** Registered new
  rents in Barcelona −6.4% from peak (−8.9% yoy at one point in 2025; stressed zones
  −3.7/−5%) per Incasòl deposit data; signed contracts **−17% to −21%** yoy in capped
  municipalities; seasonal contracts +52%. By Q4 2025–Q1 2026 average registered rents hit
  record highs again (€1,160/month; idealista on Incasòl, Feb 2026) — landlord-side Cambra
  de la Propietat calls the price effect "transitory"; the seasonal-rental loophole was
  closed in Dec 2025 and seasonal contracts fell for the first time the following quarter.
  Consistent with Pérez García: weak/temporary price effect, robust quantity effect.

## 5. Power & relations to other actors

- **vs tenants:** atomised, no individual pricing power in BdE's reading (price-taking in
  tight markets), but structural bargaining advantage at contract rotation given excess
  demand; screening (scoring, insurance, guarantors) is the main lever; multi-landlords
  in one area may hold local market power (Monràs & García-Montalvo's monopoly-distortion
  caveat).
- **vs government:** primary target of Ley 12/2023 carrot-and-stick (IRPF reductions vs
  caps); politically protected as "pequeño propietario" (85% figure in RDL 11/2020;
  ≤2-dwelling carve-out in RDL 2/2026); lobbied for by property chambers/ASVAL, opposed
  by Sindicat de Llogateres/IDRA — both sides' numbers on impago and supply effects
  diverge systematically (see §3).
- **vs banks:** low leverage; main interaction is the opportunity-cost channel (deposits/
  bonds), not credit.
- **vs large investors:** small landlords hold the habitual-residence segment; institutions
  concentrate in build-to-rent and tourist/temporada operations; no direct competition for
  the same stock in most zones (BdE: institutional share far below Germany's 34% / France's
  23%).
- **vs developers:** negligible interaction; small landlords buy existing stock
  (second-hand, inheritances).

## 7. Open questions

1. **Sell vs seasonal vs idle split.** All three Catalonia studies measure the fall in new
   tenancies but none traces where exiting units go (Pérez García explicitly leaves it to
   future research). Incasòl seasonal-contract data covers part of it; sales and idleness
   are unmeasured. The ABM needs a 3-way split parameter — currently a guess.
2. **Post-loophole behaviour (2026–).** Dec-2025 closure of the seasonal exemption removed
   the main legal escape valve; first-quarter data show seasonal contracts falling — do
   units return to residential rental, move to sale, or go informal? No data yet.
3. **Vacancy as a chosen state.** Vacancy statistics don't separate strategic withholding
   by landlords from frictional/structural vacancy (second homes, ruins, rural depopulation).
   No credible estimate of regulation-induced vacancy exists.
4. **Perceived vs actual risk gap over time.** We have one-off survey snapshots (61%
   insecurity) but no series linking perceived risk to insurance uptake, screening
   stringency, and required-yield premia. The zone-level RBA/income gradient (BdE) is the
   best proxy.
5. **Required-yield threshold.** No direct elicitation of small landlords' hurdle rates
   exists; the 3–5pp bond spread is inferred from realized spreads, not choices. Sensitivity
   analysis mandatory.
6. **Foral territories** (Basque Country, Navarre) are absent from all AEAT-based counts
   and price statistics; landlord structure there is unverified.
7. **Rent-vs-tenancy composition of the AEAT 2.15M figure** — declarant counts split
   ownership between spouses; the true number of landlord *households* is lower and only
   roughly estimable (~1.5–1.9M, unverified).
