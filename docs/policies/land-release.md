# Land release & permitting speed

Lever: increase the stock of buildable (finalist) land and/or shorten the administrative
pipeline that turns planned land into completed homes. All URLs fetched 2026-08-07.

## 1. Mechanism

**Who holds it.** Urban planning is constitutionally a CCAA competence (STC 61/1997): the
state sets only the basic property statute and land valuation rules (Ley de Suelo — today
TRLS 7/2015, historically Ley 6/1998), each CCAA writes its own land law, and municipalities
classify and zone land through the PGOU (plan general). Any "land release" intervention is
therefore executed at municipal level within CCAA law; the state can only reframe incentives
([Roca Cladera, Catastro CT-33](https://www.catastro.hacienda.gob.es/documentos/publicaciones/ct/ct33/art1.pdf);
[Burriel 2008, Scripta Nova](https://www.ub.edu/geocrit/sn/sn-270/sn-270-64.htm)).

**Pipeline.** Planned land → sector plan (plan parcial) → reparcelación → urbanización
(infrastructure) → finalist plot → building licence → construction. Claimed durations:

- Developer association (APCEspaña, via [idealista 2025](https://www.idealista.com/news/inmobiliario/vivienda/2025/05/21/841079-de-quien-es-la-culpa-de-que-falten-casas-se-tarda-hasta-20-anos-en-construir-una)):
  average ~15 years for a new sector, exceeding 20 with litigation. PGOU approval alone 6–8
  years; plan parcial + reparcelación + urbanización another 3–7 years. Examples: El Cañaveral
  (Madrid) plan parcial 2003 → first building licences 2012; La Sagrera (Barcelona) 18+ years;
  Madrid Nuevo Norte 6.5 years of planning approvals alone (2018–2024).
- Independent (IESE business school, [OP-0195-E](https://www.iese.edu/media/research/pdfs/OP-0195-E.pdf)):
  "three letters (PAU) and ten years" between zoned and developed land; Madrid's last PAUs took
  ~20 years. So the 10–20 year figure is triangulated beyond the developer lobby, though both
  sources sit on the pro-supply side; no source disputes the order of magnitude.

**Suelo finalista scarcity.** Spain has planning capacity for ~7 million dwellings on paper,
but <0.5% of available residential land is finalist (ready to build) — figure from a developer
op-ed ([Inbisa, idealista 2026](https://www.idealista.com/news/inmobiliario/vivienda/2026/04/13/891793-la-escasez-de-suelo-finalista-y-el-retraso-administrativo-el-gran-cuello-de-botella);
treat the 0.5% as an industry estimate, not official). Independent corroboration of the
bottleneck (not the exact %): [BBVA Research 2024](https://www.bbvaresearch.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/Escasez-de-oferta-residencial.pdf)
("cities have land but the finalist share is limited; development processes very long and
legally complex") and [Banco de España Informe Anual 2023 cap. 4](https://www.bde.es/f/webbe/SES/Secciones/Publicaciones/PublicacionesAnuales/InformesAnuales/23/Fich/InfAnual_2023_Cap4.pdf)
(escasez de suelo finalista listed among supply constraints; completions ~90k/yr since 2021 vs
~275k net new households/yr 2022-23; cumulative new-housing deficit ~600k units by 2025).

**Permitting (separate sub-lever).** Licencia de obra mayor: statutory ~3 months, real-world
6–18 months in Madrid depending on district; Madrid's average before the 2022 reform was 24.7
months ([ASPRIMA/Ayto. Madrid study, via Observatorio Inmobiliario](https://observatorioinmobiliario.es/noticias/residencial/la-oldruam-permite-ahorrar-hasta-50000-euros-de-media-al-comprador-de-una-vivienda/)).
Several CCAA (Madrid Ley 1/2020, others) replaced licences with **declaración responsable**
(start works on filing, ex-post control) for minor and some major works; Madrid's Ordenanza
6/2022 (OLDRUAM) made declaración responsable the default control instrument and created the
"licencia básica" and private verification entities (ECU)
([BOCM 17-05-2022](https://www.bocm.es/boletin/CM_Orden_BOCM/2022/05/17/BOCM-20220517-58.PDF)).

## 2. Evidence for

- **Geographic/regulatory land scarcity lowers supply elasticity and raises prices**
  ([Saiz 2010, QJE](https://fpeckert.me/teaching/readings/SaizQJE10.pdf), US metros): metros
  regarded as supply-inelastic are severely land-constrained; moving across the observed range
  of land availability in an average-regulated 1M metro shifts supply elasticity from ~2.45 to
  ~1.25. Land-constrained cities are more expensive ceteris paribus.
- **Planning restrictiveness has large long-run price effects**
  ([Hilber & Vermeulen 2016, Economic Journal](https://personal.lse.ac.uk/hilber/hilber_wp/hilber_vermeulen_ej_forthcoming.pdf);
  353 English LPAs, 1974–2008): ~35% of the average English house price attributable to planning
  constraints (authors' summaries call it a lower bound); South East prices ~25–30% lower under
  North East-style restrictiveness. Figures as reported in the
  [RES media briefing](https://res.org.uk/mediabriefing/house-prices-in-england-new-evidence-on-how-planning-constraints-drive-their-rapid-growth/)
  (paper PDF located; headline numbers not independently re-derived — unverified at source).
- **"Regulatory tax"** ([Glaeser, Gyourko & Saks 2003, NBER w10124](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w10124/w10124.pdf);
  [Glaeser & Gyourko 2017, NBER w23833](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w23833/w23833.pdf)):
  where regulation binds, price exceeds marginal construction cost — Manhattan >2×; gap >10% of
  house value in 9 of 21 US metros, one-third to one-half in coastal California. In lightly
  regulated growing metros price stays near production cost.
- **Spain, cross-section:** Banco de España (Informe Anual 2023, gráfico 4.11) finds
  municipalities with less available land show sharper price and rent increases under the
  post-2022 demand surge — Spanish evidence that land availability shapes price response.
- **Permitting reform, Spain:** Madrid's OLDRUAM cut average processing from 24.7 to 7.23
  months (−17.5 months; 78 days via ECU route), with 6,780 licences granted of 14,687 requested
  Jun 2022–May 2024; estimated saving ~€50k per free-market home, ~2/3 financial-carry
  ([ASPRIMA + Ayuntamiento de Madrid study](https://observatorioinmobiliario.es/noticias/residencial/la-oldruam-permite-ahorrar-hasta-50000-euros-de-media-al-comprador-de-una-vivienda/);
  [idealista summary](https://www.idealista.com/news/inmobiliario/vivienda/2024/07/08/818258-las-declaraciones-responsables-ahorran-50-000-euros-al-comprar-una-vivienda-en)).
  Caveat: authored by the developer association together with the city government that passed
  the ordinance; before/after comparison, no causal control group; effect on *completions* not
  causally evaluated anywhere we could find (no independent Spanish evaluation located —
  absence noted, not asserted).

## 3. Evidence against / side effects

- **The 1998 natural experiment ("ley del todo urbanizable").** Ley 6/1998 made all
  non-protected land residual-urbanizable with the explicit goal of cutting prices
  ([BOE](https://www.boe.es/buscar/doc.php?id=BOE-A-1998-8788)). Developable land supply rose
  ~28–30% ([Garriga 2010, FEDEA DT 2010-09](https://documentos.fedea.net/pubs/dt/2010/dt-2010-09.pdf)).
  Yet 1997–2007 average prices rose ~180% (~10.9%/yr) with starts jumping from ~248k to ~657k/yr
  ([IESE OP-0195-E](https://www.iese.edu/media/research/pdfs/OP-0195-E.pdf)); land prices rose
  ~8× nominal vs ~3× for houses, and land's share of house value went 25%→46% 1995–2008 (Uriel
  et al. 2009, cited in Garriga). Liberalization coincided with the biggest boom in Spanish
  history: the cautionary case against naive "more land ⇒ lower prices".
- **Academic decomposition — demand dominated.** Garriga's calibrated model attributes ~33% of
  the boom to immigration/demographics, ~32% to lower mortgage rates, and only ~7% to land
  deregulation; the added land quantity (+30%) should have cut unit land value ~21% but was
  swamped by demand. García-Montalvo finds **no correlation** between buildable-land
  availability and municipal house-price growth (municipalities >20k inhabitants), and argues
  land prices are *derived from* house prices (residual/negotiation channel), so land cost
  pass-through is procyclical, not causal
  ([Montalvo, "Land use regulation and housing prices"](https://jgmontalvo.com/wp/land%20use%20regulation1.pdf)).
  Expectations/credit explanations of the boom dominate the literature (García-Montalvo 2006;
  Roaldset & Støbakk 2012 [NHH thesis](http://hdl.handle.net/11250/169529)).
- **Predicted at the time:** [Roca Cladera 2000, European Planning Studies](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/713666428)
  questioned whether liberalization would lower prices and flagged territorial/sustainability
  costs; legal scholarship notes the 1998 law generated *transformable* land, not *urbanized*
  land ([Dialnet, "La intercambiabilidad del suelo"](https://dialnet.unirioja.es/descarga/articulo/3927057.pdf)).
- **Sprawl and disordered growth:** the 1997–2006 "década prodigiosa" produced exaggerated,
  developer-driven urbanization concentrated on the Mediterranean coast and Madrid's ring
  ([Burriel 2008](https://www.ub.edu/geocrit/sn/sn-270/sn-270-64.htm)); IESE independently notes
  wasteful infrastructure, councils extracting side payments for licences, and land-use
  management as a spurious municipal revenue source.
- **Speculation and corruption on rezoned land:** reclassification created windfalls for
  landowners — "the real winners" of 1997–2007 (IESE); corruption cases clustered around
  reclassification decisions ([Emerald, "Housing bubbles and land planning corruption"](https://www.emerald.com/aea/article/30/89/132/18113/Housing-bubbles-and-land-planning-corruption)
  — abstract only, effect sizes unverified).
- **Timing:** with a 10–20 year pipeline, land release delivers no short-run price relief; Banco
  de España states isolated short-term measures cannot significantly close the ~600k gap
  ([Gavilán presentation, BdE 2024](https://www.bde.es/f/webbe/GAP/Secciones/SalaPrensa/IntervencionesPublicas/DirectoresGenerales/economia/Arc/Fic/IIPP-2024-04-23-gavilan2-es-or.pdf)).
- **Overhang risk:** the land bought during the boom became a millstone on developer and bank
  balance sheets after 2008 (IESE) — mass release can amplify bust dynamics in elastic zones
  (also Glaeser-Gyourko-Saiz: elastic places overbuild more in bubbles, per Montalvo's survey).

## 4. Effect-size range

| Quantity | Range | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Supply elasticity shift from land availability | elasticity ×0.5 to ×2 across the observed land-availability range (≈1.25 ↔ 2.45 for a 1M metro) | Saiz 2010 (US); no Spanish city-level elasticity estimates located — treat Spanish values as unverified priors |
| Long-run price effect of sustained restrictiveness | 0% (Montalvo, Spanish municipal cross-section, boom years) to ~25–35% of price level (Hilber & Vermeulen, England, decades); regulatory tax 10–50% of value in constrained metros (Glaeser-Gyourko, US) | disagreement is real: keep full 0–35% span |
| Land release during a demand boom | +28–30% land supply → ~7% contribution to a ~130–180% price boom (i.e., near-zero net restraint) | Garriga 2010; García-Montalvo (null) |
| Pipeline lag, planning → finalist plot → homes | 10–20 years (developer claim 15–20; independent 10–20) | APCE; IESE |
| Permit lag (licencia de obra mayor) | 3–25 months across cities/regimes; declaración responsable ≈ 0–1 month | sector guides; ASPRIMA/Ayto. Madrid |
| Permit-lag reduction achievable by reform | −17.5 months (24.7 → 7.2) in Madrid; carry-cost saving ≈ €50k/unit (~2/3 financing) | ASPRIMA/Ayto. Madrid (interested parties, no counterfactual); effect on completions unquantified |

## 5. Model mapping

Proposed `scenario.Intervention` parameters (all randomness via the engine's seeded Generator;
disputed magnitudes stay as ranges per bias-control convention):

- `land_release_units: dict[ZoneType, int]` — finalist-equivalent plots added per year by zone
  (`tensioned_metro` / `secondary_city` / `rural`). Must pass through a delivery lag before
  entering developer buildable stock:
  - `land_release_lag_quarters: range = (20, 60)` — 5–15 years planning→finalist (uniform draw
    per scenario; 40–80 quarters if modelling from PGOU revision rather than sector approval).
- `permit_lag_quarters_delta: range = (-6, 0)` — shift in the licence stage of the construction
  pipeline; Madrid's reform ≈ −5.8 quarters is the observed maximum. Applied as a pure lag
  change on developer completions plus a carry-cost reduction on developer margin (≈ −2 to −5%
  of unit cost at current prices, from the €26.5k financial component; label: interested-party
  estimate).
- `supply_elasticity_multiplier: range = (1.0, 2.0)` — applied to the zone's new-supply price
  response once released land becomes finalist (Saiz mapping). Lower bound 1.0 (no effect)
  reflects the Montalvo/Garriga null during demand-driven episodes: the multiplier must only
  bind when developer land stock is the active constraint, not when demand/credit is.
- Side-effect hooks the model should support (from §3): landowner windfall share on rezoned
  land (speculation channel), and zero effect on prices before `land_release_lag_quarters`
  elapses — the baseline validation must show no short-run price response to land release.

Validation target: replaying 1998–2007 with `land_release_units` at +30% of zoned stock must
NOT suppress the price boom by more than ~10% (Garriga decomposition), or the supply block is
overpowered.
