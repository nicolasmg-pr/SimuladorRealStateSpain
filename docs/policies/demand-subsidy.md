# Demand subsidy (avales ICO, rent subsidies)

Researched 2026-08-07. All URLs fetched on that date; items marked (unverified) rest on
secondary press reports or search snippets not confirmed against a primary source.

## 1. Mechanism

Demand-side subsidies raise the purchasing power of a targeted buyer/tenant group without
directly adding dwellings. Standard incidence result: in markets with inelastic supply, part
or all of the subsidy capitalizes into prices/rents and accrues to sellers, developers and
landlords; the capitalized share rises with (a) supply inelasticity, (b) the share of demand
that is subsidized, and (c) how binding the credit/savings constraint was for recipients.

Spanish instruments, current and historical:

- **Avales ICO for first homes (state, 2024–)**. Line of up to €2.5bn of state guarantees
  managed by ICO with the Housing Ministry (MIVAU), approved February 2024, operational via
  bank agreements from late 2024 and extended into 2026. The State guarantees the tranche of
  the mortgage above the usual 80% LTV cap — up to 20% of the lower of price/appraisal
  (25% if the dwelling has energy certificate A–D) — so eligible buyers can borrow an
  effective 100% LTV. Guarantee lives for the first 10 years of the loan; dwelling must stay
  the habitual residence over that period; minimum loan term 5 years. Eligible: buyers aged
  ≤35 (not yet 36 at signing) and families with dependent minors (no age limit), first
  habitual dwelling in Spain, max two co-buyers. (ICO/MIVAU via idealista, fetched
  2026-08-07.) It removes the *savings* constraint (the 20% down payment), not the *income*
  constraint (affordability tests still apply, and ~10% closing costs must still be saved).
  Take-up in 2025, the first full year: ~10,450 guaranteed operations vs a government
  ambition of ~50,000 (press report, moncloa.com — unverified against ICO official data).
  For scale: Spain records roughly 600–700k home sales/year, so current take-up is <2% of
  transactions but concentrated in the young first-time-buyer segment.
- **Bono Alquiler Joven (state, 2022–, run by regions)**. €250/month rent subsidy for 24
  months, tenants aged 18–35 with income <3× IPREM, rent below a cap (originally
  €600/month, up to €900 where the region authorized it; ≤€300 for a room). The
  2026–2030 state housing plan raises it to €300/month for up to 4 years, with a rent cap
  around €1,000 (El Mundo, 2026). First call reached ~65,000 beneficiaries — about one in
  three applicants and ~0.6% of the under-35 population (Consejo de la Juventud via
  eldiario.es). Delivery has been poor: in Andalucía only 39% of 17,288 applications were
  resolved by end-2023; the regional audit office found 51.3% resolved and 25.5% never even
  processed for lack of budget, with individual waits of ~3 years (Cámara de Cuentas via
  eldiario.es; El Mundo case report).
- **Madrid "Mi Primera Vivienda" (regional, 2022–)**. Comunidad de Madrid guarantee allowing
  banks to lend above 80% and up to 100% LTV for first homes; initially ≤35, later extended
  to ≤40 years. >€60m committed 2022–2024; ~3,000 beneficiaries by Sep 2024; 4,357
  guaranteed mortgages formalized by Feb 2026; >7,000 operations by June 2026
  (comunidad.madrid; idealista). Several other regions run smaller clones.
- **IRPF purchase deduction (1979–2012, historical)**. Tax credit (~15% of amounts paid on
  the habitual dwelling, capped ~€9,015/yr of base) abolished for purchases from 1 Jan 2013
  (Ley 16/2012). Fiscal cost at peak €5–6bn/yr (~0.5% of GDP); ~€53bn over 2000–2009;
  ~€77.5bn cumulative 1979–2010 (IEF/ODF WP 6, 2012). The 2014 Lagares expert committee
  recommended phasing out the remaining transitional deduction and moving IRPF toward
  tenure neutrality; the economics literature it drew on argued the deduction was largely
  capitalized into prices (see §2).
- **IRPF rent deduction for tenants (state tranche, removed 2015)**. The state deduction for
  tenants was abolished for contracts signed from 1 Jan 2015 (Ley 26/2014 reform, in the
  wake of the Lagares committee's neutrality recommendations); regional (autonómico)
  deductions persist. The removal rationale voiced by economists was that tenant subsidies
  in a tight market pass into rents (capture by landlords); no dedicated Spanish
  quasi-experimental capitalization study of this specific deduction was located in this
  research pass — the quantitative evidence used below is international (unverified for the
  Spanish deduction specifically).

## 2. Evidence for

"For" here means: evidence that demand subsidies capitalize into prices/rents rather than
improving affordability — the mainstream critique.

- **UK Help to Buy, inelastic areas.** Carozzi, Hilber & Yu (LSE CEP DP1681, 2020; published
  Journal of Urban Economics 2024) exploit spatial discontinuities: in Greater London, HtB
  "increased house prices by more than the expected present value of the implied interest
  rate subsidy and had no discernible effect on construction volumes"; it also caused
  bunching of new units below the price threshold, smaller units, and "an improvement in the
  financial performance of developers" — i.e., local capitalization >100% of subsidy value
  and developer capture. (Abstract fetched from LSE Research Online, 2026-08-07.)
- **UK Help to Buy, deadweight and developer profits.** NAO (2019): ~three-fifths of assisted
  buyers could have bought *some* property without the scheme; ~31% could have bought the
  property they wanted; the five largest developers sold 36–48% of output through HtB and
  all increased profits since the scheme began. NAO's matched-property estimate of the price
  premium paid by HtB users is <1%, versus 5–20% in unmatched studies — a genuine
  methodological disagreement, recorded as a range, not resolved.
- **Australia FHOG.** Blight, Field & Henriquez (Deakin) conclude the grant plus the extra
  borrowing it enabled, against inelastic supply, raised the Australian median house price
  by ≈AUD 57,000 by 2008 — a multiple of the AUD 7,000–21,000 grant. The Australia
  Institute: capital-city prices grew faster in the two decades after the 2000 grant than in
  the two before; recipients "are usually close to purchasing anyway". Sydney case-study
  evidence on the 2008–09 First Home Owner Boost points the same way (Urban Policy and
  Research, 2012 — abstract only, unverified detail).
- **Spain, purchase deduction.** Ortega et al. (2011, Banco de España, cited in IEF/ODF WP 6)
  estimate that removing the IRPF purchase deduction would lower house prices by 7–8.5%
  (mean 7.8%) — i.e., the deduction was propping prices up by roughly its fiscal value.
  López García's simulation work (1996–2009) finds removal reduces both prices and the
  residential stock — ~1% for the 1998 reform exercise, up to ~16% in a full-removal
  scenario with exogenous land prices. The IEF/ODF (2012) survey states a broad consensus
  that the deduction "no favorece a las familias sino que se capitaliza", benefiting
  builders, developers and banks.
- **Rent subsidies, capture by landlords.** EsadeEcPol's review of methodologically rigorous
  studies in comparable countries (cited by its deputy director Jorge Galindo re: the Bono
  Alquiler Joven) finds landlords capture between 10% and 78% of rent subsidies via higher
  rents in new contracts. Fedea's Conde-Ruiz: "si funciona de verdad, una parte de la ayuda
  se acaba trasladando a los precios, que suben para todos". Same diagnosis from academic
  (Taltavull), think-tank (Future Policy Lab) and tenant-union voices ("un bizum a los
  caseros"). (eldiario.es 2024; El Mundo 2026; Business Insider 2024.)

## 3. Evidence against / side effects

"Against" here: evidence that qualifies the capitalization critique or documents real access
benefits.

- **Supply response in elastic areas.** The same Carozzi–Hilber–Yu design finds HtB near the
  English/Welsh border — affordable, laxer supply constraints — "did increase construction
  numbers without affecting prices". The finding flips sign with supply elasticity, which is
  exactly what the ABM's three zone types should reproduce.
- **Genuine access effects.** MHCLG's independent research (via NAO 2019): 37% of assisted
  households could not have bought *any* property without HtB; NAO estimates ~78,000
  additional new-build sales by Dec 2018; 81% of assisted buyers were first-time buyers. So
  even with substantial deadweight and some price premium, a large minority achieved a
  tenure switch they could not otherwise have made — the option value of ownership for
  credit-constrained young households (against a background where renting is often costlier
  per month than the equivalent mortgage payment).
- **Rehabilitation of rent allowances.** IEB Report (Univ. of Barcelona, 2025): per Teemu
  Lyytikäinen (VATT), "recent research does not justify a negative view of rent subsidies",
  questioning the older consensus of heavy capitalization; targeted, small-scale allowances
  may aid recipients with little market-price effect (also Auciello in eldiario.es). This
  directly opposes the 78% upper capture estimates — keep both.
- **Scale matters.** The Bono reaches ~0.6% of under-35s with €200m/call; several experts in
  the same articles who accept the inflationary mechanism doubt a program this small and
  this badly delivered moves aggregate rents. Similarly the ICO aval at ~10k
  operations/year is <2% of transactions. Capitalization pressure scales with the subsidized
  share of demand — a key experiment for the model, not a fixed fact.
- **Spanish fiscal-wedge counterpoint.** Banco de España Occasional Paper (2005, "Fiscalidad
  de la vivienda en España"): the net fiscal subsidy to ownership *declined* over 1986–2004,
  so taxation "does not appear to have contributed decisively" to the 1997–2004 price boom —
  a caution against attributing large price movements to the deduction alone.
- **Side effects of guarantee schemes.** 100% LTV loans mean maximal leverage for the
  youngest, most income-fragile buyers: negative-equity risk if prices fall (NAO flags the
  analogous new-build premium trap for HtB buyers), and contingent liabilities for the
  State. The ICO scheme is too new for any causal evaluation — only take-up data exist, and
  2025 take-up ran at roughly a fifth of the announced ambition (press, unverified). Rent
  subsidy side effects observed in Spain are administrative rather than market-level so far:
  multi-year payment delays, lottery-like competitive allocation, exclusion of the
  not-yet-emancipated (only ~17% of Spanish youth are emancipated), and rent caps that fall
  below market rents precisely in tensioned zones, shrinking effective coverage where need
  is highest (Conde-Ruiz in El Mundo, 2026).

## 4. Effect-size range

Never resolve these to points; they are the disagreement.

- **Capitalization of purchase subsidies into prices (share of subsidy value passed into
  price):** ~0–10% at the low end (NAO matched-comparison premium <1% of price for HtB
  buyers) up to **>100% of the subsidy's present value** in supply-inelastic markets
  (Carozzi–Hilber–Yu, Greater London). Unmatched UK estimates: 5–20% price premium.
  Australia: median price uplift a multiple of the grant (~AUD 57k vs AUD 7–21k grants,
  single study). Elastic-supply areas: ≈0 price effect, positive construction effect.
- **Spain, IRPF purchase deduction price effect (of removal):** −1% (López García, 1998
  reform simulation) to −7/−8.5% (Ortega et al. 2011); up to −16% in a full-removal
  exogenous-land scenario (López García). Counterpoint: BdE (2005) finds fiscal wedge not a
  decisive boom driver.
- **Rent subsidy capture by landlords (share of subsidy absorbed by higher rents):**
  **10–78%** across rigorous international studies (EsadeEcPol review); recent
  Nordic-flavored literature argues ≈0 for small, targeted schemes (IEB Report 2025). Spain-
  specific causal estimate: none located.
- **Access effect (tenure switches that would not otherwise occur):** ~37% of assisted
  purchases genuinely additional (HtB, MHCLG/NAO) — i.e., deadweight ~60–63%; Australian
  commentary suggests higher deadweight ("recipients close to purchasing anyway").
  Spain: unknown for avales ICO (no evaluation yet); Bono coverage ~0.6% of the age cohort.
- **Take-up / scale (Spain, observed):** avales ICO ~10,450 ops in 2025 (unverified press)
  of a €2.5bn line; Madrid aval ~4,400–7,000 ops 2022–mid-2026; Bono ~65,000 beneficiaries
  per call, with 25–60% of applications unresolved or unfunded in the worst region.

## 5. Model mapping

Proposed `scenario.Intervention` parameters (typed dataclass fields, units in comments):

- `guarantee_ltv_boost: float` — extra LTV made available by the state guarantee, fraction
  of price. Range 0.20–0.25 (ICO); 0.20 baseline. Raises a household's max LTV from the
  bank's `max_ltv` (0.80) toward 1.00 without changing its income-based affordability cap.
- `guarantee_eligible_share: float` — share of would-be first-time-buyer households meeting
  the age/income screen. Calibrate as a range, not a point: legal eligibility is wide
  (all <36 + families with minors) but observed take-up is narrow; sweep 0.05–0.50 of FTB
  demand to test the scale-dependence of capitalization.
- `guarantee_budget_eur: float` — line size (€2.5e9 baseline); with avg guarantee ≈ 0.20 ×
  ~€200k ≈ €40k, the line caps out around ~60k operations — a hard constraint the engine
  should enforce (guarantees granted FIFO per tick until exhausted).
- `rent_subsidy_eur_month: float` — 250 (2022–2025) / 300 (2026 plan). Paid to eligible
  tenant intents; landlords must NOT see a rule that raises rents — any capture must emerge
  from clearing (landlords re-optimizing asking rents given observed willingness to pay).
- `rent_subsidy_duration_months: int` — 24–48.
- `rent_subsidy_eligible_share: float` — sweep 0.006 (observed Bono reach) to 0.20
  (French-APL-like generalized allowance) of under-35 tenant households.
- `rent_subsidy_rent_cap_eur: float` — 600–1,000; eligibility lapses where market rent
  exceeds the cap, so coverage endogenously collapses in the tensioned-metro zone as rents
  rise (an observed Spanish failure mode worth reproducing).
- Optional friction: `subsidy_admin_delay_ticks: int` — 0–36 months; Andalucía shows delays
  large enough to nullify the policy for planning purposes.

Capitalization is an OUTPUT, never an input. Measure in `metrics.py`:

1. `subsidy_price_capitalization` — (Δ mean transaction price for subsidized buyers vs
   matched unsubsidized counterfactual run) ÷ subsidy present value; compute per zone.
   Validation target: near 0 in the rural (elastic) zone, ≥0.5 and possibly >1 in the
   tensioned metro, echoing Carozzi et al. vs NAO range.
2. `rent_subsidy_capture` — Δ market rent in new contracts ÷ subsidy amount, per zone;
   plausibility band 0.10–0.78 in tensioned zones at meaningful eligible shares, ≈0 at the
   0.6% observed scale.
3. `access_effect` — share of subsidized purchases/tenancies by households that fail the
   unassisted down-payment constraint in the counterfactual run (target order: ~0.35–0.45
   genuine additionality, per HtB).
4. `deadweight_share` — 1 minus (3) among recipients.
5. Distributional: change in seller/developer surplus vs buyer surplus per € of subsidy.
6. Risk: share of guaranteed loans in negative equity under a price-fall scenario.

Interaction experiments the evidence motivates: subsidy × supply elasticity (zone type),
subsidy × eligible share (scale threshold at which capitalization kicks in), and subsidy
with/without simultaneous supply-side policy.
